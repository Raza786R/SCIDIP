Critical Bottlenecks and Mitigation Strategies for an
AI-Driven  Inverse  Design  Platform  Targeting  High-
Entropy Metal-Ceramic Gradient Architectures
Summary For  ML  model  selection,  a  hybrid  approach  is  recommended:  use
composition-only models like transformer-based or stacking ensemble models for
initial high-throughput screening of millions of compositions, as they do not require
exact 3D structures. For the Bayesian Optimization under data scarcity, implement a
multi-fidelity  active  learning  loop  with  a  hierarchical  surrogate  model  (e.g.,  low-
fidelity empirical rules, medium-fidelity ML predictions, high-fidelity FEA) and use
acquisition  functions  like  Expected  Improvement  or  Knowledge  Gradient  to
strategically  select  candidates  for  expensive  FEniCS  simulations.  Critical  workflow
drawbacks include the severe computational cost and potential numerical instability
of  running  10,000  thermal  cycles  in  FEniCS,  the  risk  of  error  propagation  from
oversimplified  empirical  rules  in  early  screening,  and  the  physical  limitations  of
continuum  models  in  capturing  atomic-scale  interface  phenomena.  Mitigations
involve  reduced-order  modeling  for  FEA,  uncertainty  quantification,  and  a  tiered
validation protocol.
1.  ML  Model  Selection  for  High-Throughput  Property  Prediction  in
Refractory High-Entropy Alloys
The core challenge in predicting properties for millions of unsimulated high-entropy alloys
(HEAs) in a 5-element refractory system (Mo, Nb, Ta, W, Cr) is the fundamental trade-off between
model accuracy and data availability. This section analyzes the primary modeling approaches,
focusing on their applicability given the absence of defined 3D atomic structures for synthetically
generated compositions.
1.1 Limitations of Structure-Dependent Models like ALIGNN
Atomistic graph neural networks, such as ALIGNN, require precise 3D crystal structure inputs
(e.g., in .cif format) to generate accurate property predictions. This presents an insurmountable
barrier for the initial high-throughput screening of fractional compositions, as these alloys lack
defined atomic configurations. Generating Special Quasi-random Structure (SQS) supercells for
millions of compositions to feed into ALIGNN would be computationally prohibitive, as the cost
scales with the number of elements and atoms involved1. Therefore, while powerful for well-
characterized systems, ALIGNN is not viable for the initial screening stage of this inverse design
pipeline.

1.2 Viability of Composition-Only and Empirical Models
Given  the  structural  data  bottleneck,  composition-only  models  or  those  using  empirical
descriptors offer a more practical path for initial screening.
Empirical Rules for Phase Stability: Miedema's semi-empirical rules are a established tool
for predicting single-phase body-centered cubic (BCC) stability in refractory HEAs2 1 3. They
provide  a  computationally  cheap  first-pass  filter.  However,  their  limitations  are  well-
documented; they rely on approximations and may omit critical thermodynamic and kinetic
factors, leading to potential inaccuracies in complex multicomponent systems2 3. Their use
should be tempered with an understanding of these constraints.
Machine Learning on Compositional Features: Models like Roost or CrabNet, which predict
properties directly from elemental descriptors and compositions, are better suited for high-
throughput  screening.  They  bypass  the  need  for  atomic  coordinates.  Their  accuracy,
however, is inherently limited by the quality and scope of their training data. For the vast,
unexplored  5-element  compositional  space,  data  scarcity  is  a  severe  issue,  risking  poor
model generalizability and extrapolation errors.
1.3 Evaluating Workarounds: Virtual Crystal Approximation and SQS
For compositions that pass initial screening, higher-fidelity property prediction (e.g., for bulk
modulus, thermal conductivity) requires some structural approximation.
Virtual Crystal Approximation (VCA): VCA averages atomic potentials over a surrogate BCC
lattice. It is highly efficient but becomes inaccurate for systems with significant atomic size
mismatch or lattice distortion, as it cannot capture local atomic environments. For the Mo-
Nb-Ta-W-Cr system with moderate atomic radius variations, VCA may introduce substantial
errors in enthalpy and predicted phase stability1.
Special Quasi-random Structure (SQS): SQS supercells explicitly model disorder and are
necessary for accurate property prediction when atomic size differences matter. The cost of
generating and computing properties via density functional theory (DFT) for SQS models
scales unfavorably with the number of components and atoms, making it impractical for
screening  millions  of  compositions1.  It  is  best  reserved  for  a  small  subset  of  the  most
promising candidates.
1.4 Recommended Hybrid, Tiered Strategy
A pragmatic solution employs a multi-stage workflow that balances computational cost with
predictive fidelity.
Tier 1: High-Throughput Compositional Screening
Method: Use a composition-only ML model (e.g., Roost/CrabNet) trained on available
refractory HEA data, supplemented by predictions from empirical rules like Miedema's
enthalpy criteria.

This tiered strategy naturally integrates with an active learning framework to combat data
scarcity. The ML models in Tier 1 can be designed to quantify their own prediction uncertainty.
Compositions  with  high  uncertainty  (e.g.,  those  near  predicted  phase  boundaries)  or  high
expected performance can be prioritized for the expensive SQS/DFT calculations in Tier 3. The
results from these targeted high-fidelity simulations are then fed back to retrain and improve the
Tier 1 screening models, creating a closed-loop, data-efficient discovery process.

2.  Bayesian  Optimization  Framework  Design  under  Extreme  Data
Scarcity
2.1 The Challenge of Data Scarcity and BO's Suitability
The design of high-entropy alloys (HEAs), particularly for the five-element Mo-Nb-Ta-W-Cr
refractory system, is fundamentally constrained by extreme data scarcity. Acquiring experimental
or first-principles Density Functional Theory (DFT) data is prohibitively expensive, and the vast
dimensionality  of  the  five-element  compositional  space  renders  traditional  trial-and-error
methods  inefficient.  Bayesian  Optimization  (BO)  is  a  sequential  optimization  framework  that
uses a surrogate model and acquisition function to guide efficient exploration with a minimal
number of evaluations, making it particularly suitable for such data-scarce scenarios.

under data scarcity by strategically integrating information sources of varying cost and accuracy.
This  approach  balances  computational  expense  against  predictive  fidelity.  A  representative

2.3 Surrogate Model Selection and Training Strategy
The choice and training of the surrogate model is critical under data scarcity. While Gaussian
Processes (GPs) are a standard choice for BO due to their inherent uncertainty quantification,
they  face  challenges  in  high-dimensional,  mixed-variable  spaces  (containing  both  continuous
compositions and categorical structural descriptors).
Adaptive Surrogate Strategies:
Latent Variable Gaussian Process (LVGP): For design spaces with mixed variables, the LVGP
model  maps  categorical  variables  to  a  continuous  latent  space  to  improve  modeling,
demonstrating effectiveness under extreme data scarcity 67.
Ensemble  and  Adaptive  Models: To  enhance  robustness,  ensemble  methods  or  more
adaptive  Bayesian  surrogate  models,  such  as  Bayesian  multivariate  adaptive  regression
splines  or  Bayesian  additive  regression  trees,  can  be  employed.  These  may  offer  better
performance for high-dimensional or non-smooth objective functions common in materials
design 8.
Physically  Constrained  Gaussian  Processes: Incorporating  known  physical  constraints
(e.g., thermodynamic stability bounds) into the GP prior can improve model reliability and
extrapolation even with scarce data 9.
Training Data Strategy:
Active Learning Loop: The BO framework itself functions as an active learning loop. The
acquisition  function  selects  compositions  expected  to  provide  the  maximum  information
gain  (e.g.,  to  reduce  prediction  uncertainty  or  improve  the  optimum),  ensuring  each
expensive FEA evaluation is maximally informative.
Cost-Aware  Sampling: In  a  multi-fidelity  setting,  algorithms  like  Cost-Aware  Gradient
Entropy Search (CAGES) can systematically identify samples that maximize information gain
about  the  objective  function  per  unit  of  evaluation  cost,  which  is  crucial  for  efficient
optimization 10.

2.4 Acquisition Function Design and Exploration-Exploitation
Under data scarcity, the design of the acquisition function must carefully balance exploring
unknown regions of the space and exploiting known promising areas.
Adaptive Acquisition Strategies:
Expected Improvement (EI) / Upper Confidence Bound (UCB): These standard functions
can be effective but may require tuning for the specific landscape.
Local  BO  for  High  Dimensions: For  high-dimensional  problems,  local  BO  methods  like
TuRBO,  which  maintains  a  collection  of  local  models,  can  offer  more  efficient  global
optimization by focusing exploration adaptively 11.
Multi-Objective  Acquisition: Since  the  target  "Interface  Failure  Risk"  is  a  composite  loss
function, it may be beneficial to decompose it into multiple objectives (e.g., minimize stress,
maximize  conductivity,  ensure  stability).  Multi-objective  BO  using  an  acquisition  function
like  Expected  Hypervolume  Improvement  (EHVI)  could  then  identify  a  Pareto  frontier  of
optimal trade-offs.
2.5 Workflow Integration and Computational Efficiency
For the specific goal of optimizing a metal-ceramic gradient architecture, the BO framework
must be integrated into a hierarchical workflow.
Hierarchical  Optimization  Architecture: A  practical  workflow  begins  with  generating
millions of fractional compositions, followed by a low-cost filter for BCC single-phase stability
using empirical rules like Miedema's (acknowledging their documented limitations in complex
multi-component systems 213). The filtered set is then evaluated by a fast, composition-only ML
surrogate model (e.g., Roost/CrabNet) to predict bulk properties. The BO loop operates on this
pre-screened  space:  its  surrogate  model,  trained  on  accumulated  FEA  results,  suggests  new
candidate compositions. These candidates are first vetted by the low-cost filters and ML model
before the most promising are passed to the expensive FEniCS FEA for the final, high-fidelity
evaluation of the loss function. The results are fed back to update the BO surrogate, closing the
loop.
Efficiency Optimization:
Parallel/Batch Evaluation: Batch BO variants allow for the parallel evaluation of multiple
candidates,  which  is  essential  for  leveraging  high-performance  computing  resources  and
mitigating the time cost of FEA simulations.
Search Space Pruning: Rigorous application of stability filters (mixing enthalpy, atomic size
mismatch) based on available facts  2 4 5 prunes the search space to physically plausible
regions, preventing wasted computation on unstable alloys.

2.7 Implementation Challenges and Mitigation
Key challenges in this data-scarce environment include:
Surrogate Model Misspecification: With little data, an inappropriate model choice can lead
the optimization astray. Mitigation involves starting with flexible models (like LVGP), using
ensemble methods for robustness 128, and incorporating any available physical priors 9.
Cold Start Problem: Initializing the BO loop requires some seed data. This can be addressed
by using the highest-confidence predictions from the physics/ML screening stage as initial
points, or by employing meta-learning techniques to transfer priors from related material
systems if such data exists 1314.
Validation  of  Low-Fidelity  Filters: The  accuracy  of  the  initial  Miedema/ML  screening  is
paramount. Their predictions must be periodically validated against high-fidelity results to
prevent the systematic exclusion of promising regions of the composition space.

3.2 Validity and Limitations of the Physical Model
The  physical  models  within  FEniCS  face  fundamental  limitations  for  long-term  thermal
fatigue simulation.
1. Temperature-Dependent Material Constitutive Behavior The mechanical behavior of
high-entropy  alloys  at  1400K  is  significantly  different  from  room  temperature.  For  instance,
studies on CoCrFeMnNi alloys show yield stress and tensile strength decrease with increasing
temperature,  while  elongation  increases18.  This  temperature-induced  softening  and  plastic
enhancement must be accurately captured. For the Mo-Nb-Ta-W-Cr system, a critical bottleneck
is the lack of high-temperature constitutive data, forcing reliance on extrapolated or idealized
models.
2. Assumption of Continuity at the Gradient Interface FEniCS typically assumes material
properties vary continuously within elements. However, the metal-ceramic gradient interface is
discontinuous at the micro-scale. This continuum assumption fails to capture:
Atomic-scale diffusion and interfacial reactions.
Nano-scale stress concentrations and defect formation.
Mismatched time scales for phase transformation and precipitation kinetics.
3. Cumulative Errors from Cyclic History Effects The cumulative error over 10,000 cycles
may render results non-physical. Long-term material degradation mechanisms like oxidation and
creep  are  often  not  considered18.  Furthermore,  microstructural  evolution  processes  such  as
grain  growth  occur  on  time  scales  much  longer  than  the  simulation  time  step  and  are  not
captured by standard continuum models.
3.3 Numerical Stability and Convergence Challenges
The  strongly  nonlinear  nature  of  the  coupled  thermo-elastic  problem  leads  to  numerical
stability issues. A staggered iterative solution scheme (updating temperature, then displacement/
stress fields, then temperature-dependent properties) may diverge under conditions prevalent in
this simulation:
Large Thermal Gradients: A ΔT > 500K at the interface can cause singularities due to abrupt
changes in the coefficient of thermal expansion.

Computational Acceleration:
Reduced-Order  Modeling  (ROM):  Employ  techniques  like  Proper  Orthogonal
Decomposition (POD) to create fast surrogate models, reducing cost by orders of magnitude
for the initial high-throughput screening.
Multi-Fidelity Modeling: Combine low-fidelity, fast models for screening with high-fidelity
FEniCS simulations for final validation of top candidates.
Adaptive  Workflows:  Implement  an  active  learning  loop  where  a  lightweight  surrogate
model  pre-screens  the  vast  compositional  space,  and  the  expensive  FEniCS  simulation  is
invoked  only  for  the  most  promising  or  uncertain  designs  identified  by  Bayesian

4.  Validity  and  Limitations  of  Empirical  Rules  for  High-Throughput
Screening
4.1 Fundamentals and Scope of Empirical Screening Rules
In the design of high-entropy alloys (HEAs), empirical rules such as Miedema's rules, Hume-
Rothery  rules,  and  valence  electron  concentration  (VEC)  criteria  are  widely  used  for  initial
screening.  These  rules  provide  simplified  criteria  for  predicting  phase  stability  in  multi-
component alloy systems based on fundamental physicochemical properties of the constituent
elements.
Miedema's rules predict the likelihood of solid solution formation by calculating parameters
like  mixing  enthalpy  (ΔHmix)  and  atomic  size  mismatch  (δ).  For  refractory  HEAs  (RHEAs),
particularly in five-element systems like Mo-Nb-Ta-W-Cr, these rules are often applied to forecast
body-centered  cubic  (BCC)  single-phase  stability3.  Hume-Rothery  rules  focus  on  atomic  size
difference (typically <15%), crystal structure match, electronegativity difference, and VEC. As an
extension,  VEC  rules  correlate  phase  structure  with  electron  concentration:  alloys  with  VEC
between 5.7–7.2 predominantly form BCC structures, while those with VEC ≥ 8.4 tend to form
face-centered cubic (FCC) structures20.
4.2 Inherent Limitations and Accuracy Challenges
Despite  their  computational  simplicity  and  speed,  which  are  advantageous  for  high-
throughput  screening,  the  inherent  limitations  of  empirical  rules  become  pronounced  in
complex multi-component systems.
1. Oversimplification and Neglect of Local Environment Effects Methods like Miedema's
model and the Virtual Crystal Approximation (VCA) treat an alloy as a "virtual" metal by averaging
elemental properties. This approach has critical limitations: it ignores local atomic environment

They  cannot  capture  non-linear  interactions  and  synergistic  effects  in  multi-component

Their  extrapolation  capability  is  poor  for  new  alloy  systems  that  deviate  from  the  data

high-quality training data and often lack interpretability21.
3. Neglect of Kinetics and Non-Equilibrium Processes Empirical rules are primarily based
on thermodynamic equilibrium assumptions, overlooking critical kinetic factors in real synthesis:
They cannot account for the influence of processing parameters like cooling rate or defect
density on phase stability.
They  ignore  the  formation  mechanisms  of  metastable  phases  under  non-equilibrium
conditions.
For  instance,  under  rapid  cooling,  a  predicted  "stable  BCC  phase"  might  undergo
amorphization or other phase transformations not captured by the rules4.

2. Blind Spots in Phase Stability Prediction
Empirical  rules  cannot  predict  short-range  order  (SRO),  which  significantly  influences  the

They  ignore  contributions  from  magnetic  and  vibrational  entropy,  which  are  crucial  for

3.  Specific  Challenges  for  Gradient  Interface  Systems In  metal-ceramic  gradient
architectures, empirical rules face additional hurdles:
The chemical gradient at the interface leads to local composition variations, invalidating the
uniform composition assumption underlying the rules.
Stress fields generated by the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) mismatch affect phase

4.5 Risk Assessment of Catastrophic Physical Drawbacks
Relying  solely  on  empirical  rules  for  initial  screening  carries  the  risk  of  several  critical
physical failures:
1. Risk of Phase Stability Misjudgment
Incorrectly  predicting  single-phase  stability  could  lead  to  the  formation  of  harmful
intermetallic compounds during actual synthesis.
Ignoring temperature-dependent phase transformations may result in phase decomposition
under high-temperature service conditions (e.g., 1400 K)4.
Phase stability changes in oxidizing or corrosive environments are not considered.
2. Neglect of Interface Failure Mechanisms
Empirical  rules  cannot  predict  diffusion-controlled  phase  transformations  at  the  gradient
interface.
They ignore stress-induced phase transformations during thermal cycling.
The formation and evolution of interfacial reaction layers are not predictable.
3. Systemic Error in Property Prediction
Property  predictions  (e.g.,  thermal  conductivity,  elastic  modulus)  based  on  empirical
correlations may contain systemic biases.
The  influence  of  microstructure  (grain  boundaries,  dislocations)  on  properties  is  not
captured.
The complex process-structure-property relationship is overlooked.

data flow bottlenecks between machine learning predictions and finite element analysis (FEA),
gradient  interface  modeling  complexities,  and  the  amplification  of  uncertainties  through  the
multi-stage pipeline.

The core strategy involves using a lightweight, multi-fidelity surrogate model. This model
would be trained initially on all available data, including low-fidelity sources like empirical rules
and medium-fidelity CALPHAD predictions. The BO loop then operates in cycles:
The  surrogate  model  predicts  the  objective  function  (Interface  Failure  Risk)  and  its  own

An acquisition function, designed for data scarcity, selects the next batch of compositions to

maximum information gain to improve the model for future iterations.
5.3  Critical  Workflow  Drawbacks:  Error  Propagation  and  Computational
Scalability
Several  physical  and  computational  drawbacks  threaten  the  workflow's  validity  and
scalability, potentially leading to catastrophic errors in the final lifetime predictions.
1.  Limitations  of  Empirical  Rules  for  Initial  Screening Relying  on  empirical  rules  like
Miedema's model for the initial BCC single-phase stability filter is a significant vulnerability. The
provided  facts  consistently  note  that  while  useful,  these  rules  have  inherent  limitations  in
complex  multicomponent  systems2 1 3.  They  are  based  on  approximations  and  omit  key
thermodynamic and kinetic factors3. An alloy passing these filters may not be truly stable under
the non-equilibrium conditions of processing or the extreme thermal cycling at 1400K. Phase
stability  in  refractory  high-entropy  alloys  is  highly  temperature-dependent,  with  single-phase
solid solutions often stable only at high temperatures4 5. Using oversimplified empirical criteria
risks  propagating  fundamentally  unstable  compositions  deep  into  the  workflow,  wasting
computational resources and yielding misleading results.
2. Computational Scalability of FEniCS Thermal Cycling Simulations The plan to simulate
10,000 thermal cycles at 1400K for gradient architectures with FEniCS presents a severe, possibly
prohibitive, computational challenge. The FEA of a spatially graded 5mm architecture with five
distinct metal compositions blended into a ceramic requires a highly refined 3D mesh to resolve
stress  concentrations  at  the  interface.  Each  thermal  cycle  is  a  transient,  non-linear  coupled
thermo-mechanical simulation. Performing this analysis 10,000 times for a single composition
candidate is extraordinarily computationally intensive. Scaling this to even dozens of candidates
from the BO loop would demand immense high-performance computing resources, creating a
major bottleneck that limits the number of compositions that can be realistically evaluated.

3. Amplification of Uncertainty Through the Pipeline Errors introduced at early stages do
not  remain  contained;  they  amplify  through  subsequent  stages.  A  composition  incorrectly

Mitigation Strategies for Robust Integration To address these drawbacks, the workflow
must be redesigned with uncertainty and efficiency as core principles:
Uncertainty Quantification: Embed uncertainty estimates at every stage (stability filter, ML
prediction, BO surrogate) and propagate these uncertainties through the pipeline to the final
FEA result.
Tiered Fidelity Screening: Implement a stricter, multi-tiered screening process before FEA.
After  the  empirical  filter,  use  rapid,  lower-fidelity  models  (e.g.,  analytical  thermo-elastic
models) to further down-select candidates. Reserve the full 10,000-cycle FEniCS simulation
only for the absolute top-tier candidates that pass all previous checks.
Physics-Guided  ML: Use  physics-based  constraints  and  known  stability  data  for  related
systems5 to  guide  and  constrain  the  ML  property  prediction  models,  improving  their
reliability when extrapolating to novel compositions.
Reduced-Order  Modeling  for  FEA: Explore  reduced-order  models  or  surrogate  models
trained on a smaller set of full FEA simulations to approximate the 10,000-cycle outcome for
a larger set of BO candidates, reserving the full simulation for final validation.
The  integration  challenges  highlight  that  the  workflow's  success  depends  not  just  on
individual  component  accuracy,  but  on  a  coherent  strategy  to  manage  computational  cost,
quantify uncertainty, and prevent error propagation across the multi-scale design pipeline.

The  challenge  of  predicting  properties  for  millions  of  fractional  HEAs  without  exact  3D

To enhance the robustness of these predictions and address data scarcity, the ML models

of  Miedema's  rules—such  as  approximations  in  complex  multicomponent  systems—must  be
acknowledged, and their predictions should be treated as guiding priors rather than definitive
truths23.
For  compositions  that  pass  the  initial  ML  screening  and  require  more  reliable  property
estimates  (e.g.,  elastic  modulus,  thermal  conductivity),  a  tiered  atomistic  modeling  approach
should  be  employed.  The  Virtual  Crystal  Approximation  (VCA)  provides  a  computationally
efficient method for rapid property estimation by treating the alloy as an average crystal. For
systems with constituent elements of similar atomic radii and electronic configurations, VCA can
offer  reasonable  accuracy.  For  compositions  exhibiting  significant  atomic  size  mismatch  or
where greater fidelity is needed, Special Quasirandom Structures (SQS) should be generated to
better capture local lattice distortions. The decision to use VCA or SQS can be guided by simple
compositional  thresholds,  creating  an  adaptive  protocol  that  optimizes  computational  cost
against required accuracy.

The  core  of  this  strategy  is  a  hierarchical  surrogate  modeling  approach.  A  low-fidelity
surrogate,  such  as  the  composition-only  ML  model  described  above,  can  screen  millions  of
points. Promising candidates from this screening are evaluated with a medium-fidelity model
(e.g., VCA or SQS calculations). Only the most optimal or uncertain compositions from this tier
are passed to the high-fidelity, expensive FEniCS FEA simulations3233. This multi-fidelity setup
ensures that the costly FEA is reserved for a minimal set of truly promising designs.

6.3 3. Computational Workflow Optimization and Validation
The  proposed  workflow  faces  significant  scalability  challenges,  particularly  in  the  FEniCS
FEA simulation of 10,000 thermal cycles on a spatially graded architecture. Relying solely on full-
order simulations for this task is computationally prohibitive.
A primary mitigation strategy is the adoption of reduced-order modeling techniques for the
thermal cycling analysis. Instead of simulating every cycle at full resolution, an adaptive cycle
selection protocol can be implemented. A small number of initial cycles can be simulated in
detail to capture transient behavior, followed by simulations at increasingly sparse intervals once
the response stabilizes. The behavior for intermediate cycles can be interpolated, with periodic
full-resolution checkpoints for validation. This can reduce the number of full solves by one to two
orders of magnitude.
The spatial gradient itself presents both a challenge and an opportunity for optimization.
The  5mm  gradient  architecture  can  be  exploited  via  domain  decomposition  techniques.  The
domain  can  be  split  into  subdomains  of  more  homogeneous  material,  allowing  for  coarser
meshing  in  bulk  regions  and  fine  meshing  only  at  the  critical  metal-ceramic  interface  where
stress  concentrations  occur.  Parallel  computing  strategies,  such  as  MPI-based  domain
decomposition, are essential to leverage high-performance computing resources effectively for
these large-scale, nonlinear thermo-mechanical simulations.
Finally, the validity of the entire workflow, which hinges on empirical rules and surrogate
models for screening, must be rigorously addressed. A multi-scale validation hierarchy is crucial.
Predictions from the ML surrogates and atomistic models (VCA/SQS) should be validated against
any  available  higher-fidelity  data,  such  as  DFT  results  or  CALPHAD  calculations  for  select
benchmark compositions5. Most importantly, the workflow must include strategic experimental
validation.  A  carefully  chosen  set  of  10-20  compositions,  spanning  the  predicted  Pareto  front
from  the  BO,  should  be  synthesized  and  characterized.  This  provides  ground-truth  data  to
calibrate  models,  validate  critical  interface  property  predictions,  and  ultimately  verify  the
platform's design recommendations24.
